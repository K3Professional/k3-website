You found the perfect job, hit Submit, and heard nothing back. It's easy to assume someone read your resume and passed. More often, no one read it at all.

Most employers now run applications through an applicant tracking system (ATS) before a recruiter ever sees them. The software scans for relevant keywords, readable formatting, and a clear match to the job. Resumes that don't pass get filtered out quietly. The ones that do pass then get a quick skim from a busy hiring manager.

That means your resume has two audiences: a machine and a human. Here are five mistakes that lose one or both of them, and how to fix each one.

## 1. Sending the Same Resume to Every Job

A one-size-fits-all resume saves time, but it costs interviews. Every job description uses its own language, and the ATS is looking for that language specifically.

**The fix:** Before you apply, read the job posting closely and pull out the skills and terms it repeats. If the posting asks for “project management” and “vendor relations,” those exact phrases should appear in your resume wherever they honestly describe your experience. Move your most relevant experience toward the top, where both the software and the reader will find it first.

## 2. Using a Format the Software Can't Read

That creative two-column template with icons and text boxes might look great to you. To an ATS, it can look like scrambled text. Tables, graphics, headers and footers, and unusual fonts can cause the system to skip or misread your information entirely.

**The fix:** Use a clean, single-column layout with standard section headings like “Experience,” “Education,” and “Skills.” Stick to common fonts, use simple bullet points, and leave enough white space for a human reader to scan quickly. Save the design flair for your portfolio.

## 3. Listing Duties Instead of Results

Your job description tells an employer what you were supposed to do. Your achievements tell them what you actually did, and what you could do for them.

Compare these two lines:

> Managed customer service calls.

> Cut average customer response time from 48 hours to 12 by redesigning the call-routing process.

The second line gives the reader proof, scale, and a reason to call you. Whenever you can, include a number: time saved, revenue grown, costs reduced, people trained, or projects delivered. If you don't have exact figures, a reasonable estimate (“about 30 clients a week”) is still stronger than no number at all.

## 4. Including Everything You've Ever Done

A resume isn't a career autobiography. Every line that doesn't support the job you're applying for pushes the lines that do further out of sight.

**The fix:** Keep what's relevant and cut the rest. For most people, that means focusing on the last 10 to 15 years, keeping each role to three to five strong bullet points, and dropping outdated skills. If an older role built a skill this job needs, keep it, but frame it around that skill.

## 5. Letting Small Errors Slip Through

A typo won't always cost you the job, but it can cost you the benefit of the doubt. Spelling mistakes, inconsistent dates, mismatched punctuation, and shifting fonts make a resume look rushed, and employers read that as a signal about how you work.

**The fix:** Proofread more than once. Read your resume out loud to catch awkward phrasing, check that every date and job title follows the same format, and ask someone you trust to review it with fresh eyes.

## Before You Hit Submit

Run through this quick checklist:

- Does my resume use the same key terms as this job posting?
- Is the format simple enough for an ATS to read?
- Do my bullet points show results, ideally with numbers?
- Is everything on the page relevant to this role?
- Have I proofread it, and has someone else?

If you answered no to any of these, you've found your next edit.
