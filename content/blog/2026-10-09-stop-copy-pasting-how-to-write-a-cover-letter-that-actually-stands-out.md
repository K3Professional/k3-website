Most cover letters start the same way: “I am writing to express my interest in the open position…” If yours does too, you're not alone. Templates feel safe, and staring at a blank page is hard.

But when a hiring manager reads that same opening line dozens of times a day, it blends into the pile. Your cover letter shouldn't just repeat your resume. It should tell the story your resume can't.

Here's how to write one that gets read, remembered, and gets you to the interview.

## 1. Do Your Homework First

The best cover letters are written about the company, not just to it. Before you write a word, spend 15 minutes learning what the company cares about right now.

- Read the job description closely and note the problems the role is meant to solve.
- Check the company's website, recent news, and LinkedIn posts for new projects, goals, or announcements.
- Look for the hiring manager's name while you're on LinkedIn.

Those details become the raw material for everything that follows.

## 2. Open With Value, Not an Introduction

Your first sentence is your best chance to earn attention. The employer already has your name from the application, so skip the introduction and lead with what you bring.

Instead of this:

> “My name is Alex, and I am applying for the Marketing Specialist position at your company.”

Try this:

> “Most marketing teams measure success in impressions. I measure it in customers. Last year, I rebuilt a stalled content pipeline and grew qualified leads by 35%, and I'd love to do the same for your team.”

The second version tells the reader who you are as a professional and gives them a reason to keep reading.

## 3. Focus on What They Need, Not What You Want

It's natural to explain why a job appeals to you. But employers are reading your letter to answer one question: how will this person help us? Shift the focus from your goals to theirs.

| Instead of this | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| “I'm looking for a place where I can grow my sales skills and learn from industry leaders.” | “My account management experience in fast-moving markets can help your team reach its fourth-quarter expansion goals.” |
| “This role aligns perfectly with my personal interest in sustainable tech.” | “Your new green supply chain initiative caught my eye. I spent the last year streamlining the same kinds of supply routes you're building.” |

Notice that both stronger examples depend on the research from step 1. Specific details show you're not sending the same letter to everyone.

## 4. Tell the Story Behind Your Best Result

Your resume lists facts. Your cover letter brings one of them to life. Choose a single achievement that closely matches the job description and walk through it in three parts:

1. What problem did you or your team face?
2. What did you do, specifically, to solve it?
3. What changed, ideally in numbers? For example: “The new workflow saved our team 12 hours a week.”

One well-told story is more convincing than a list of five skills.

## 5. Close by Asking for the Conversation

Many cover letters fade out with “Thank you for your consideration.” End with confidence instead. Restate what you'd bring, and invite the next step:

> “I'd welcome the chance to talk about how I can help your team grow qualified leads this year. Thank you for your time, and I look forward to speaking with you.”

## 6. Keep It Short and Easy to Skim

Hiring managers often skim before they decide to read closely, so make your letter easy to take in at a glance.

- **Aim for under 300 words.** Three or four short paragraphs is plenty.
- **Use bullet points sparingly.** Two or three bullets highlighting your most relevant wins can draw the eye right where you want it.
- **Write like a person.** Skip buzzwords like “synergy,” “dynamic team player,” and “go-getter.” Plain, confident language stands out more.

## Before You Send: A Quick Checklist

- **Did I address a real person?** A name is best. If you truly can't find one, “Dear Hiring Team” is a friendly alternative to “To Whom It May Concern.”
- **Did I mention something specific about this company?** If your letter could be sent to any employer, it needs more tailoring.
- **Did I ask for the interview?** Your closing should invite the next conversation.
- **Is it a PDF?** Saving as a PDF keeps your layout clean on any screen.
- **Does it match my resume?** Use the same font, sizing, and header so your application looks polished and consistent.
