You're sitting in the interview. You've shaken hands, settled into your seat, and everything feels like it's going well. Then the interviewer leans back and says:

> So, tell me about yourself.

Your mind goes blank. Where do you even start?

Here's the good news: the interviewer isn't asking for your life story. They're giving you the floor to introduce yourself professionally and show why you're a strong fit for the role. With a simple structure and the right kind of practice, this question can become one of your strongest moments in the interview.

## Use the Present–Past–Future Approach

A simple way to organize your answer is to move through three points:

1. What are you doing now?
2. What experience prepared you for this opportunity?
3. Why are you interested in this position?

Here's what that sounds like:

> Right now I'm a customer service lead, and I'm usually the person my team hands the toughest calls to, because I'm good at calming people down and actually solving the problem. Before that, I spent three years in retail, where I learned how to read customers quickly and keep things moving on busy days. Now I'm looking for a role where I can take that experience and start training and supporting a team, which is a big part of why this position caught my attention.

Notice what makes it work. It's short, it follows a clear path, and it includes a specific detail that sticks. “I'm the one who gets the toughest calls” says far more than “I have strong communication skills.”

## Keep It Relevant

You don't need to walk through every job you've ever had. Focus on the experiences, skills, and accomplishments that connect to the position you want.

Your interviewer already has your resume, so don't just read it back to them. Tell the story behind it: what you've learned, what you're good at, and what led you to this opportunity.

Your starting point shapes what you emphasize:

- **Changing careers?** Lead with transferable skills.
- **Recent graduate?** Draw from your education, internships, class projects, volunteer work, or part-time jobs.

For example, a teacher moving into corporate training might say:

> For the past six years, I've taught high school English, which means I've spent a lot of time explaining complex ideas to people who didn't necessarily want to hear them. I've also designed my department's onboarding program for new teachers, and that's the work I've enjoyed most. That's why I'm excited about moving into corporate training, where I can focus on helping adults build new skills.

## How to Avoid Sounding Rehearsed

Preparing your answer is smart. Memorizing it word for word is where people get into trouble. A memorized answer tends to sound flat, and if you lose your place, it's hard to recover. Here's how to prepare without sounding scripted:

- Write down three or four key points, one for your present, past, and future. Practice from those notes so the ideas stay fixed while the wording stays natural.
- Each time you practice, phrase it a little differently. That way, you're learning the ideas instead of the exact words.
- A specific example, number, or moment makes your answer sound like a conversation instead of a speech.
- Say your answer to a friend or record yourself on your phone. You'll quickly hear where you ramble or sound stiff.
- A closing line like “That's what drew me to this role, and I'd love to hear more about what the team is working on” turns your answer into a conversation.

## Keep It Short

Aim for one to two minutes. Think of your answer as an introduction, not your full professional history. If the interviewer wants more detail, they'll ask, and that's a good sign.

## Before Your Interview

Take a few minutes to answer these questions:

- What do I most want the interviewer to know about me?
- Which of my experiences are most relevant to this job?
- What's one specific detail or result I can include?
- Why am I genuinely interested in this opportunity?

Once you have those answers, you have your key points, and the rest comes down to practice.
